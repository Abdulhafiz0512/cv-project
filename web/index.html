Every traffic event at one Tashkent junction, on a timeline.

A fixed 4K camera looks over a T-junction. Our system reports each violation or incident it sees as a time segment with one of 14 classes, and raises an accident-risk alarm using only the frames seen so far.

Approach

A pretrained detector and a tracker turn the video into trajectories; everything after that is rules on those trajectories and the scene layout. We did not train a model: without labels for this camera, rules we can inspect beat a classifier we cannot validate.

  1. Decode

    PyAV skips the B-frames of the camera's IBBP stream: 10 frames per second of 4K at 1.3 times the speed of a full decode, on its own thread, downscaled to 1280×720.

  2. Detect

    YOLO26-S at 1280 px finds cars, buses, trucks, motorcycles, bicycles, pedestrians and animals.

  3. Track

    ByteTrack links boxes into trajectories. Riders and drivers are folded into their vehicle so they never count as pedestrians.

  4. Normalise

    The ground point is the bottom centre of a box; speeds are in box heights per second, which cancels most of the perspective of this elevated view.

  5. Apply rules

    One rule per class on trajectories and the scene layout. The inbound signal faces away from the camera, so red is read from a vehicle waiting at the stop line.

  6. Shape segments

    A strict condition must hold for a minimum time; the segment then spans the looser condition around it, merged per class, to match the annotation conventions at IoU 0.7.

Accident risk (Part B)

A separate, causal pass at 5 frames per second: ByteTrack online, then four cues fused into one probability. Footprints of road users on crossing paths that are about to overlap; hard braking with someone close ahead; wrong-way motion; and crossing the stop line while another vehicle waits. The score rises at once and decays with a two-second time constant.

Report

A short, honest account of how the project went: what we built, the problems we hit on the way, and what we still have not solved.

What we built

We did not train a model. With no labels for this camera, we decided early that rules we can read and test would beat a classifier we could not validate. So the system is a pretrained detector (YOLO26-S) and a tracker (ByteTrack) that turn the video into trajectories, then one rule per event class that looks at those trajectories against a map of the junction. The map is fitted to the paint and kerbs of one video and warped onto every other video after registering its view, because the framing changes between recordings. Part B is a separate pass that only ever sees past frames and scores how close road users are to colliding. In the end we ran the whole pipeline, through the organizers' unchanged harness, on all four sample videos: 18 minutes of footage, 87 events, every video inside the time limit.

What went wrong, and what we changed

We could barely get the data. The four sample videos are raw camera files, 2.3 to 6.2 GB each, and our laptop had about 3 GB of free disk. On top of that, Google Drive answered full downloads with "Quota exceeded", because every team was pulling the same links. We found that small byte-range requests still went through, so we wrote a little proxy that feeds ffmpeg in bounded chunks and transcodes on the fly. It got us the first 12 seconds of C3905 before Drive closed completely. Most of the development was done on those 12 seconds plus synthetic trajectories in our tests. Later the full C3896, C3902 and C3905 files arrived on a machine with enough disk, and C3897 as a 720p copy (the resolution the pipeline works at anyway), so every result on this page now comes from the complete videos.

Our first run was over the time limit. The 12-second clip took 37.3 s against a 36.1 s budget. We assumed the detector was the problem, but profiling showed it was decoding: the camera writes 4K, 10-bit, 4:2:2 video at about 147 Mbit/s, and the organizers' harness decodes every frame again for Part B. On our laptop that harness decode alone runs at 0.75× real time. We cut our own decode cost by asking the decoder to skip B-frames (the stream is IBBP, so we still get 10 frames per second), which took Part A's decode from 1.22× to 1.61× real time. Loading the model while the harness imports our module, before its timer starts, saved a few more seconds.

The risk score fired all the time. Our first Part B used time-to-collision between points, and on a normal 12 seconds of traffic it averaged 0.46 and peaked at 0.90, which would have been a stream of false alarms. The camera sits high and looks along the road, so two cars in neighbouring lanes look almost touching in the image. We switched to checking whether the ground footprints of two road users on crossing paths are about to overlap. On the same clip the score now stays at 0.

Parked cars "saw" a red light. The inbound signals face away from the camera, so we read red from behaviour: a car waiting at the stop line. The first version counted cars parked on the left strip as waiting cars, so the whole clip looked red. We added a parking zone to the map.

The bus and the queue. stopped_vehicle first flagged the bus dwelling at its stop, then cars creeping in a queue. When we added a rule that a car standing next to other stopped cars is a queue, it went the other way and hid a car that really was stopped for the whole clip, because the bus next to it counted as "the queue". A dwelling bus is no longer a queue neighbour.

People beside the zebra. Pedestrians at the east crossing walk a metre or two next to the paint, not on it, and our first road outline there was drawn too wide. Both produced jaywalking alarms. We fixed the outline and made the zebra tolerance scale with each person's height.

A bug that would have cost everything. A review of the code before submission found that the near-miss rule used a NumPy call that raises an error on 2-D vectors in NumPy 2. Any video with a car braking hard would have crashed Part A and been scored as empty. We fixed it and now run every rule in isolation, so one broken rule cannot take the others down.

A courier is not a car. The only failure_to_yield on our clip turned out to be a delivery rider moving slowly along a zebra with pedestrians. Two-wheelers now need riding speed to count, and a person moving faster than about 4 m/s is treated as a scooter rider, not a pedestrian.

The stop line was in the wrong place. On the first full video the red stop line in our render ran through the zebra instead of along the paint in front of the waiting cars. Two things were wrong. Our map had been drawn on the C3905 clip, and even there its far end was 14 px low. And the camera is not framed the same way twice: C3896 is zoomed and panned against C3905 by about 25 px, and C3902 differs by more than 60 px. The organizers call it the same camera and angle, so the test videos will move too. We refitted the map to the markings of C3896 by fitting lines to the paint on a background with the traffic removed. That fixed the stop line, the zebras (the south one is a fan, wider towards the camera, not a rectangle) and an island that was 35 px off. Then we stopped treating the map as fixed. Each video is matched to two reference backgrounds (a day one and a dusk one) on its first frames with SIFT features, and the map is warped into that video's view. It takes a quarter of a second per video. Warping the C3905 paint into C3896 lands within half a pixel of where we measured it directly, and on C3902, the most shifted video, the stop line, zebras and islands all land on the paint.

Every car was the same colour. Our review video coloured boxes by object type, so every car was yellow, and there was no way to see which car an event was about. Each rule now records the road users it fired on, and the render draws that one thick, in the event's colour, with the event's name. We also found the review video ran about 1.3 times too fast, because the decoder hands over frames unevenly; it now holds each frame on a fixed clock and plays in real time. Both changes are what made the next step possible.

Watching our own renders found more false alarms than any test. We went through every long or clustered event on all four videos.

  • red_light: every one was a car in the lane by the median. That lane moves on its own arrow: in C3896 all 20 of its crossings happened while the other lanes were waiting, while the other lanes almost never crossed with anyone waiting (at most 4 of their 43 crossings). Red evidence now only counts from a car in the same phase group.
  • stop_line: most were cars held past the line because the junction ahead was jammed. The rule now needs a red for its lanes and no stationary car directly in front.
  • illegal_u_turn: all five were fake. Cars creeping in a queue have a jittery heading that adds up to 150°, and a car entering under the camera has its box cut by the frame, which drags its ground point sideways. Headings now come only from clearly moving cars fully inside the frame.
  • failure_to_yield and near_miss: the same frame-edge effect. A box shrinking as a car leaves the picture looks like hard braking, and one cut by the bottom edge puts the car on the zebra. Both rules now ignore boxes that touch the border, and a near miss needs a pedestrian or a moving vehicle ahead, not a parked car.
  • jaywalking: we suspected the far kerb by the bus stop, where people stand still for a long time. On the frames the kerb in our map was right; one man really was standing out in the road, so we left the rule alone.

On C3896 these fixes took the output from 51 events to 20. Every one we removed was a false alarm we had looked at; the ones that stayed include a car driving through the west zebra while pedestrians crossed on red.

What worked

Measuring speed in "box heights per second" instead of pixels was the most useful single idea: it cancels most of the perspective, so one threshold for "standing still" works near the camera and at the far end of the road. Reading the signal phase from waiting cars works where the lamps are not visible. Keeping a strict condition for the core of an event and a looser one for its edges gives segment boundaries that follow the annotation rules instead of the moment a threshold trips. Running the detector at 1280 px mattered: at 960 px it found 42% fewer pedestrians (15.1 vs 26.1 per frame), mostly the crowd at the far bus stop.

Registering each video to a reference view made the map trustworthy on videos we had never measured. Measuring lanes across the direction of traffic, not along the stop line, separated the five inbound lanes cleanly (57 px apart, with clear gaps), which is what let us see the median lane's own phase. And the single most productive tool was a render that shows exactly which road user each event is about: every rule fix in the last round came from watching it.

What did not

We still have no labelled dev set, so we cannot give you an F1 score, and every threshold is our best judgement checked by eye on the renders rather than a tuned value. failure_to_yield is our most frequent class (43 of 87 events); the cases we checked were a mix of drivers who really did not yield and borderline ones that only labels can settle. The risk score's highest peak, 0.87 on C3902, was a delivery motorbike cutting in front of a car: a real conflict, but not a collision, so the metric counts it as a false alarm. C3897 is only available to us as a 720p copy. We do not predict illegal_turn or fire_smoke: we could not make them reliable from this view, and the metric punishes predicting a class that never occurs. The accident and near-miss rules have never seen a real collision from this camera. And the line between "crossing next to the zebra" and "jaywalking" is still a judgement call.

What we would do next

First, label the full sample videos and tune every rule against the official metric at IoU 0.7. Then add background modelling for stopped vehicles and obstacles, which is what the AI City Challenge winners used, and a small open vision-language model to double-check the rare accident and fire candidates; one around 2B parameters fits the 5 GB weight limit and the T4 if it is only called a few times per video. A proper ground-plane calibration from the zebra and lane markings would give us speeds in metres and a better risk score.
